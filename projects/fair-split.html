fair-SPLIT: Near-Optimal Decision Trees for Accuracy, Sparsity, and Fairness

Statistical Machine Learning, course project (team of 2)  ·  Spring 2026

Code  ·  Report

Overview

Greedy tree induction such as CART is prone to local optima, and exhaustive global search is prohibitively expensive at scale; meanwhile, feature bias inherent in certain datasets can degrade both predictive performance and fairness. Building on the SPLIT family of algorithms, this project studies a hybrid search framework that performs optimal lookahead over a shallow prefix of the tree while recursively completing the remaining subtrees—striking a trade-off between global optimality and computational tractability, and producing sparse trees amenable to fairness auditing.

Across five random seeds on 12 benchmark datasets, SPLIT-family models are competitive with CART on several tasks and outperform it on selected datasets such as COMPAS, HELOC, Heart, and Law School, while CART remains strongest on many standard accuracy benchmarks. For group fairness, removing sensitive attributes before training is not sufficient on its own; label-aware sample-level post-processing sharply reduces demographic-parity gaps, while Leaf-Pareto Fair Recalibration (LPFR) provides a more conservative, deployable leaf-level adjustment.

Background & Motivation

Why interpretable trees. Decision trees are among the most widely deployed models because every prediction is traceable through a short sequence of human-readable splits—exactly what high-stakes domains such as healthcare, criminal justice, and credit lending demand for auditability.

Two shortcomings of greedy induction. CART and C4.5 make myopic split choices that optimize local impurity reduction with no guarantee on global structure, and small lookahead errors at the top of the tree propagate downward. Greedy search also exhibits a well-documented feature selection bias toward features with many distinct values, which can silently encode sensitive attributes and amplify discrimination against protected subgroups.

Why not just solve for the optimal tree. Globally optimal tree induction would mitigate both issues, but the problem is NP-hard, and exact solvers such as GOSDT and MurTree do not scale to large datasets. The SPLIT framework offers a principled middle ground: exact dynamic-programming search over the top layers of the tree—where splits carry the highest discriminative leverage—combined with cheap completion of the remaining leaves.

Method

Objective. SPLIT formulates tree learning as regularized risk minimization: minimize the training error plus a sparsity penalty λ·|L(T)| on the number of leaves. When λ = 0 the objective reduces to empirical risk; as λ → ∞ the optimal tree collapses to a single leaf, so sweeping λ traces the full Pareto frontier between error and complexity. The exact solver operates on binarized features (midpoint thresholds or GBDT stump–based threshold guessing) to keep the DP state space tractable.

Phase 1 — optimal prefix search. Given a total depth budget d and lookahead depth dl < d, a dynamic program with memoization constructs the optimal depth-dl prefix under the regularized objective. Three accelerations keep the search practical: memoization on the hashed label vector and depth, optional top-k feature pre-filtering ranked by entropy gain, and branch-and-bound using greedy completion as an upper bound at the lookahead boundary.

Phase 2 — leaf filling. Each prefix leaf is completed to depth d − dl, either greedily (a split is accepted only if the sum of regularized child losses is strictly less than the leaf loss) or optimally by re-invoking the DP solver per leaf. Two extensions enrich the family: LicketySPLIT, a polynomial-time variant with lookahead dl = 1, and ReSPLIT, which samples diverse near-optimal trees from the ε-Rashomon set and reports the tree with the lowest regularized risk.

Fairness pre-processing. The first intervention removes all sensitive attribute columns (e.g., sex, race) before training, eliminating direct use of the protected attribute at negligible computational cost. Indirect discrimination via proxy features is not automatically removed, so the remaining fairness behavior is evaluated empirically.

Sample-level calibration (diagnostic). A label-aware post-processing step flips hard predictions within each group toward the global positive rate, prioritizing already-misclassified examples (false positives first when reducing a group's rate, false negatives first when raising it). It runs in O(N) with no retraining, but because it uses ground-truth labels to choose flips, its results are an optimistic diagnostic rather than a deployable inference-time rule.

Leaf-Pareto Fair Recalibration (deployable). Proposed LPFR as the deployable counterpart: the trained tree structure is frozen, leaves are identified on a calibration split, and the method iteratively selects the leaf-level prediction override with the best fairness-gain-to-accuracy-loss ratio, continuing until any further override would exceed a user-specified accuracy budget. Because overrides attach to leaves, LPFR can be applied at inference time using only the sample's path through the tree. Sparsity regularization, ReSPLIT diversity, sample-level calibration, and LPFR are orthogonal and composable, letting practitioners explore the accuracy–sparsity–fairness Pareto surface without expensive constrained optimization.

Experiments

Datasets. 12 public classification benchmarks, six of which carry sensitive attributes for fairness evaluation: Adult (sex, race), Bank (marital status, age), COMPAS (sex, race), German Credit (personal status, age), Law School (race, gender), and ACSIncome (sex, race). The remaining six—HELOC, Spambase, Covertype, Thyroid, Communities, and Heart—support accuracy and efficiency comparisons; Covertype is 7-class and Communities is binarized at the median target.

Protocol. All datasets use an 80/20 train–test split with stratification when feasible, and all reported numbers are means and standard deviations over five random seeds. In fairness mode, sensitive columns are removed before training, and one of three regimes is applied: no post-processing, sample-level calibration, or LPFR.

Baselines. CART (Gini impurity, max depth 5) is compared against SPLIT-greedy (lookahead dl = 2, depth d = 5, λ = 0.0001, greedy Phase 2, top-50 feature cap), SPLIT-optimal (same configuration, optimal Phase 2), and ReSPLIT (N = 30 prefixes, ε = 0.02).

Metrics. Predictive accuracy (%), training time (s), and three group-fairness metrics computed from test-set hard predictions: Statistical Parity Difference (SPD, the maximum gap in positive prediction rates across groups; 0 is ideal and values above 0.1 are conventionally considered unfair), Disparate Impact (DI, the ratio of smallest to largest group positive rate; 1 is ideal, and below 0.8 indicates adverse impact under the four-fifths rule), and Equal Opportunity Difference (EOD, the maximum gap in true positive rates; 0 is ideal). Groups with fewer than 50 test samples are excluded as statistically unreliable.

Results

Accuracy: no single learner dominates. CART leads on 8 of 12 datasets, but SPLIT-family methods win where it counts: SPLIT-optimal on COMPAS (66.38 ± 0.53% vs. CART's 66.10 ± 0.78%), SPLIT-greedy on Law School (95.02 ± 0.07% vs. 94.93 ± 0.14%), and ReSPLIT on HELOC (70.26 ± 2.31% vs. 68.14 ± 0.80%) and Heart (78.69 ± 2.32% vs. 76.39 ± 2.49%)—the largest positive margin for the family. The largest negative margin appears on Covertype, where CART reaches 70.16 ± 0.19% while the SPLIT variants remain near 67%.

Efficiency: SPLIT-greedy occupies the low-runtime region. SPLIT-greedy is the fastest method on 8 of 12 datasets thanks to candidate-feature control, making it the most attractive SPLIT-family option in practice. ReSPLIT is consistently slower because it trains many candidate trees—the gap is extreme on Adult (1743.5 s vs. CART's 6.6 s), Covertype, and ACSIncome. Fairness post-processing does not uniformly change accuracy; when accuracy moves, the direction depends on the dataset and the method.

Fairness: attribute removal alone is not enough. Without post-processing, the best uncalibrated statistical-parity gap remains high on Adult (0.1744 ± 0.0116), COMPAS (0.2248 ± 0.0215), and ACSIncome (0.1671 ± 0.0223), confirming that proxy features and dataset imbalance persist after sensitive columns are dropped.

Calibration: an optimism–deployability trade-off. Label-aware sample-level calibration nearly eliminates the parity gap on all six fairness datasets (best average SP between 0.0013 and 0.0044; 0.17 → 0.003 on Adult), but it is a diagnostic upper bound. LPFR is more conservative yet deployable: it nearly closes the gap on Law School (best average SP 0.0001 ± 0.0003) and Bank (0.0072 ± 0.0059), substantially improves COMPAS (0.1554 vs. 0.2248 base) and ACSIncome (0.1181 vs. 0.1671 base), but does not close the gap on Adult (0.1424 vs. 0.1744 base). Equal-opportunity results remain mixed—LPFR improves average EO on Bank, COMPAS, and Law School, whereas demographic-parity–oriented sample calibration can worsen EO on ACSIncome, a reminder that parity does not enforce equal opportunity.

Conclusion & Limitations

Takeaways. fair-SPLIT combines the SPLIT family with lightweight fairness interventions to study accuracy, sparsity, and demographic parity jointly. No single tree learner dominates: CART is strongest on many conventional accuracy benchmarks, while SPLIT-optimal, SPLIT-greedy, and ReSPLIT each lead on selected datasets. Fairness improvements are similarly method- and dataset-dependent: sensitive-attribute removal is insufficient on its own, sample-level calibration nearly eliminates statistical parity as an optimistic diagnostic, and LPFR provides the more conservative but genuinely deployable post-processing mechanism.

Limitations. Sample-level calibration uses ground-truth labels to select flips, so its near-zero gaps are an upper bound rather than an inference-time guarantee. LPFR cannot close the parity gap when the learned tree never isolates the affected subgroups, as on Adult, and demographic-parity gains do not imply equal-opportunity gains (EO stays high on ACSIncome). ReSPLIT's cost grows substantially on larger datasets, and fairness-aware model selection from the Rashomon set is left as a downstream step that can be composed with LPFR in future work.
